How a PC works (in plain English)

Skip this if you already know what a CPU is. Otherwise: ~5 min read. Each part of the shopping list maps to one box below.

A desktop PC is really just six things wired together: a brain (CPU), a graphics worker (GPU), short-term memory (RAM), long-term storage (SSD), a power supply, and a motherboard that connects everything. The case holds it all. That's the whole story — the rest is detail.

CPU — the brain

Think: a chef who can do anything, one task at a time per "burner."

The CPU runs the operating system, your apps, the game logic, and physics. "Cores" are independent burners — a 6-core chip can run 6 threads of work in parallel. Clock speed (GHz) is how fast each burner cooks.

In this build: The Ryzen 5 5600 has 6 cores. For a 3070 at 1080p/1440p that's plenty — the GPU will be the bottleneck, not the CPU.

GPU — the graphics worker

Think: 5,000 line cooks who can only chop vegetables, but in parallel.

The GPU is specialized for one thing: doing the same simple math on millions of pixels at once. That's what 3D games are — math on pixels. It's also why GPUs got hijacked for AI: training a neural net is the same shape of work.

In this build: Luccas' RTX 3070 has 5,888 cores and 8 GB of its own memory (VRAM). That's why the build is "case + parts" — the expensive piece is already owned.

RAM (DRAM) — the desk

Think: the desk space the chef spreads ingredients on while cooking.

RAM holds whatever the CPU is actively working on — open apps, browser tabs, the game currently running. It's fast (~50 GB/s) and volatile (empty when you power off). More RAM = more apps open without slowdown. Speed (DDR4-3200) matters less than having enough.

In this build: 32 GB DDR4-3200 — comfortable for gaming + a browser with 80 tabs + Discord + Spotify, all at once.

VRAM — the GPU's private desk

Think: the line cooks have their own counter, separate from the chef's desk.

The GPU has its own memory glued onto the card. Why? Because shipping textures and frame data over the bus to system RAM would be way too slow. The 3070's 8 GB of VRAM holds all the textures and shadow maps for the current scene.

In this build: 8 GB VRAM is fine at 1080p/1440p in 2026. At 4K with ray tracing on, modern AAA games can spill over 8 GB — that's the 3070's main limit.

Storage — the filing cabinet

Think: the pantry. Slow vs. desk, but it remembers everything when the lights go out.

Storage holds files that survive a power-off: your OS, games, photos. Two flavors:

  • HDD (hard drive): physical spinning platter + a needle. Cheap, big, slow (~150 MB/s). Mostly dead in 2026 except for backups.
  • SSD (solid-state drive): flash chips, no moving parts. Fast (500–7000 MB/s) and silent.
    • SATA SSD — older connector, ~550 MB/s. Fine for older laptops.
    • NVMe SSD — gum-stick stick that plugs straight into the motherboard via PCIe. 3,500–7,000 MB/s. This is what the build uses.

In this build: 1 TB WD Blue SN570 NVMe. Boot times in seconds, games install in minutes.

PSU — the power supply

Think: the kitchen's electrical panel — converts wall power into the voltages each part needs.

Wall outlets give 120 V AC; PC parts want 12 V / 5 V / 3.3 V DC. The PSU does the conversion and splits the load. Wattage (e.g. 650 W) is the maximum it can deliver. 80+ Gold means it's ≥87% efficient — the rest leaks as heat.

In this build: 650 W SFX (the small form factor). A 5600 + 3070 pulls ~350 W under load, so 650 W is comfortable headroom.

Motherboard — the wiring

Think: the kitchen counter + outlets + plumbing. It doesn't do work, it connects everything.

The motherboard is the green PCB that everything plugs into. CPU socket, RAM slots, PCIe slot for the GPU, M.2 slot for the SSD, USB ports, network port, BIOS chip. It also delivers clean power to the CPU via small VRMs (voltage regulators).

Form factor is just the size of the board: ATX (big), Micro-ATX (medium), Mini-ITX (tiny — what this build uses). Smaller board = smaller case = the whole point of SFF.

The bus / I/O — how parts talk

Think: the highways between rooms.

Components don't just touch — they talk over buses: shared lanes that move data.

  • PCIe — the big highway. The GPU sits on a 16-lane PCIe slot. NVMe SSDs sit on 4 lanes. Newer = faster (PCIe 3.0 → 4.0 → 5.0, each doubling speed).
  • DDR — the road from CPU to RAM. DDR4 / DDR5.
  • SATA — old highway for hard drives & older SSDs. ~600 MB/s cap.
  • USB — the road for external stuff. USB-A, USB-C, Thunderbolt.

Every spec like "PCIe 4.0 ×16" is just which highway, how many lanes, which generation.

CPU cooler — keeping the brain cool

Think: a radiator on top of the chef's head.

CPUs turn ~65–105 W of electricity into heat. Without a cooler they'd hit 100 °C in seconds and throttle (slow themselves down to survive). Two flavors:

  • Air cooler — heatpipes carry heat to fins, a fan blows air through them. Cheap, reliable, no leaks.
  • AIO (all-in-one liquid) — a closed loop where coolant moves heat to a radiator with fans. Cools better at the same noise level, costs more, can leak (rarely).

In this build: The cooler is the tricky part of SFF. Big air coolers don't fit in small cases. The picker above warns you when the chosen cooler is too tall for the chosen case.

How a frame of a game gets to your monitor

1. CPU runs game logic         → "the player just moved 2 ft forward"
2. CPU tells GPU what to draw  → over PCIe, ~16 GB/s
3. GPU reads textures          → from its own VRAM (~448 GB/s on a 3070)
4. GPU does the math           → millions of pixel ops in parallel
5. GPU writes the finished frame to a buffer in VRAM
6. The frame goes out the DisplayPort/HDMI cable to your monitor
7. Repeat 60–144× per second

Power flows: wall → PSU → motherboard + CPU + GPU + SSD. Heat flows out: CPU/GPU → cooler → case fans → room.
